Short window: 3 seasons, 38 games. Error bars are wide, and the takeaways say so.

Vanderbilt has spent 3 seasons beating expectations: 19 wins against 16.5 expected. The question is how. Were the expectations too low, with the public slow to believe in a good team? Did the bounces go its way, winning games the play did not quite earn? Or did the roster outplay its recruiting rankings? The sections below take those one at a time, split them by head coach, then ask whether any of it changes as a season goes on. Each ends with a short takeaway; a note under it says how much to trust the number. 3 seasons and 38 games for Vanderbilt.

Terms used on this page
Expected margin
How many points the team was expected to win or lose by, according to the point spread the sportsbooks posted just before kickoff. It sums up what oddsmakers, bettors, and the public expected, with money behind it, so this page uses it as the measure of expectations. A week-earlier version (the opening number) also exists; when the two differ, opinion moved during the week.
Expected wins
Each game's expected margin converted to a chance of winning, then added up. The record the public thought was coming.
Deserved wins
Each game's postgame win probability, from CollegeFootballData's play-by-play model, added up. How many games a team that played that way usually wins. The gap between actual and deserved wins is what most people call luck.
SP+
Bill Connelly's efficiency rating of how well a team actually played, adjusted for opponent. Used here as the measure of quality, separate from the record.
Talent composite
247Sports' rating of the whole roster's recruiting pedigree. Used here as the measure of what the players were supposed to be.
Beat expectations (by)
Won by more than the expected margin, or lost by less. "Beat by" is the actual margin minus the expected margin: +7 means seven points better than expected. A bettor would say the team covered.
One-score game
Decided by eight points or fewer.
Head coach, tenure year
The head coach who worked the most games that season, and how many seasons into his run at the school it was, counting seasons before this page's window and the 2020 season.
Standard error
The size of gap that random variation alone produces about a third of the time. A number within one standard error is noise. Beyond two, it is probably real. Each takeaway has a "How sure is this?" note that says which.

1. Were the expectations wrong?

Every game carries an expected margin, the point spread posted just before kickoff, which sums up what oddsmakers, bettors, and the public thought would happen. Convert each one to a chance of winning and add them up, and you get the record the public expected. Deserved wins come from the other direction: after each game, a play-by-play model estimates how often a team that played that way wins it. If the public expected more wins than the play deserved, the expectations were too optimistic.

One standard error on this gap is about ±1.8 wins over 38 games, so -1.9 is suggestive but not conclusive (1.0 standard errors).

4. Does it change as the season goes on?

Each faint line is one season, game by game: how many points Vanderbilt beat expectations by, or fell short. The heavy line is the 3-season average for that game of the year. The table pools the seasons into early, middle, and late stretches. The expected margin already moves week to week as opinion changes, so a pattern here says the public was slow to adjust, in one direction or the other.

Takeaway: By margin, Vanderbilt has faded as seasons go on: 1.3 points a game ahead of expectations from game 9 on, versus 8.0 points a game ahead of expectations in the first four. Because the expected margin already adjusts week by week, that means the public kept believing longer than the play warranted. In wins: 8-4 early against 7.6 expected (+0.4), 5-9 late against 4.8 expected (+0.2).

How sure is this?

One standard error on the early-versus-late margin difference is about ±5.7 points a game (12 early games, 14 late), so -6.7 is suggestive but not conclusive (1.2 standard errors). On the wins comparison one standard error is about ±2.0 wins, so -0.2 is within the noise (0.1 standard errors). Single-game swings of 20 points are routine, which is why the faint season lines look so jagged.

Bottom line: Expectations for Vanderbilt have probably run too low (-1.9 wins, suggestive but not conclusive). Luck has been a non-factor (+0.6 wins, within the noise). The roster has outplayed its rankings by SEC standards (-3 spots against an SEC average of -10).
