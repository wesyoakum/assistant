Georgia has spent 9 seasons beating expectations: 109 wins against 100.4 expected. The question is how. Were the expectations too low, with the public slow to believe in a good team? Did the bounces go its way, winning games the play did not quite earn? Or did the roster outplay its recruiting rankings? The sections below take those one at a time, split them by head coach, then ask whether any of it changes as a season goes on. Each ends with a short takeaway; a note under it says how much to trust the number. 9 seasons and 128 games for Georgia, 2020 left out.

Terms used on this page
Expected margin
How many points the team was expected to win or lose by, according to the point spread the sportsbooks posted just before kickoff. It sums up what oddsmakers, bettors, and the public expected, with money behind it, so this page uses it as the measure of expectations. A week-earlier version (the opening number) also exists; when the two differ, opinion moved during the week.
Expected wins
Each game's expected margin converted to a chance of winning, then added up. The record the public thought was coming.
Deserved wins
Each game's postgame win probability, from CollegeFootballData's play-by-play model, added up. How many games a team that played that way usually wins. The gap between actual and deserved wins is what most people call luck.
SP+
Bill Connelly's efficiency rating of how well a team actually played, adjusted for opponent. Used here as the measure of quality, separate from the record.
Talent composite
247Sports' rating of the whole roster's recruiting pedigree. Used here as the measure of what the players were supposed to be.
Beat expectations (by)
Won by more than the expected margin, or lost by less. "Beat by" is the actual margin minus the expected margin: +7 means seven points better than expected. A bettor would say the team covered.
One-score game
Decided by eight points or fewer.
Draft capital, draft rank
NFL draft picks produced in the draft after the season, weighted 5 for round one, 3 for round two, 2 for round three and 1 after that, ranked nationally. Every pick credits the player's final college season. It is the outside world's verdict on how good the players who played actually were.
Who played
The average 247 recruiting rating of the players who took the snaps: offensive skill players weighted by their share of the team's plays, defenders by tackles and other involvements, and the five highest-rated linemen presumed to have played because box scores do not record linemen. Compared with the roster's top 44 to show whether the best-rated players were the ones on the field.
Head coach, tenure year
The head coach who worked the most games that season, and how many seasons into his run at the school it was, counting seasons before this page's window and the 2020 season.
Standard error
The size of gap that random variation alone produces about a third of the time. A number within one standard error is noise. Beyond two, it is probably real. Each takeaway has a "How sure is this?" note that says which.

1. Were the expectations wrong?

Every game carries an expected margin, the point spread posted just before kickoff, which sums up what oddsmakers, bettors, and the public thought would happen. Convert each one to a chance of winning and add them up, and you get the record the public expected. Deserved wins come from the other direction: after each game, a play-by-play model estimates how often a team that played that way wins it. If the public expected more wins than the play deserved, the expectations were too optimistic.

How sure is this?

9 seasons of rank averages carry roughly ±4 spots of noise; single-season gaps of 20 or more spots are well outside it. The peer comparison is the fair one, since the talent composite is generous to every roster here.

Takeaway: By what the NFL took, the roster ranked No. 13 on average against No. 3 by recruiting pedigree: 75 players drafted over 9 seasons, 20 in the first round, most from the 2021 team (15 picks). Against a field average of -8 that is typical: the recruits became pros about as their rankings promised, by the standards of the programs here. The players who actually took the snaps rated 0.936 on the 247 scale (four-star), against 0.965 for the roster's top 44. Lower-rated players than the roster's best got the snaps more than at most programs here (-8 thousandths against the field average): either the top recruits did not win jobs or the ratings were wrong about who could play.

How sure is this?

Draft rank is the national rank of round-weighted draft picks (5 for round 1, 3, 2, then 1) in the draft after each season, so it credits every pick to the player's final season. One standard error on a 9-season rank average is about ±4 spots. "Who played" weights offensive skill players by season usage share (5% of plays or more counts in full), defenders by tackles plus sacks, tackles for loss, passes defended and interceptions (15 or more counts in full), and presumes the five highest-rated linemen played because the box score cannot see linemen. Ratings were known for 92% of that weight; the rest, mostly walk-ons, are filled in at the roster's lowest-quartile rating.

4. Does it change as the season goes on?

Each faint line is one season, game by game: how many points Georgia beat expectations by, or fell short. The heavy line is the 9-season average for that game of the year. The table pools the seasons into early, middle, and late stretches. The expected margin already moves week to week as opinion changes, so a pattern here says the public was slow to adjust, in one direction or the other.

Takeaway: By margin, Georgia has finished seasons better than it started them: 2.1 points a game ahead of expectations from game 9 on, versus 1.4 points a game short of expectations in the first four. Because the expected margin already adjusts week by week, that means the public was slow to catch up. In wins: 33-3 early against 30.2 expected (+2.8), 45-11 late against 41.0 expected (+4.0).

How sure is this?

One standard error on the early-versus-late margin difference is about ±3.3 points a game (36 early games, 56 late), so +3.4 is suggestive but not conclusive (1.0 standard errors). On the wins comparison one standard error is about ±3.5 wins, so +1.2 is within the noise (0.3 standard errors). Single-game swings of 20 points are routine, which is why the faint season lines look so jagged.

Bottom line: Expectations for Georgia have been about right (-3.0 wins, within the noise). It has clearly been lucky (+5.6 wins, unlikely to be noise). The roster has outplayed its rankings by SEC standards (-5 spots against an SEC average of -13).
